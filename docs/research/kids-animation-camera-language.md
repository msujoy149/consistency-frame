# Kids Animation — Camera Language Reference

The full camera system for the `kids-family-animation` skill. Use it with the 8-question decision system: no angle, shot size, movement, or lens is chosen without answering the eight questions first. The camera follows the story, the emotion, and the physical action — never random cinematic angles merely to look complicated.

## Angles

- **Eye-level** — camera at the character's eye height. Neutral, honest, the default for conversation and everyday scenes.
- **Low-angle** — camera below the subject, looking up. The subject feels big, powerful, or scary. Villain, mountain, triumph.
- **High-angle** — camera above, looking down. The subject feels small, weak, or lost. A character in trouble, a first look at a new place.
- **Bird's-eye / top-down** — camera directly above. Layout of a place, a chase, a pattern. Use sparingly; it stops the story to show geography.
- **Worm's-eye** — camera on the ground, looking straight up. The world towers over the character. A small character facing something huge.
- **Dutch-angle** — camera tilted. Something is wrong, funny, or off-balance. Use briefly; a whole scene tilted is tiring.
- **Over-the-shoulder** — behind one character's shoulder, looking at the other. Conversation, secret, decision. The foreground shoulder anchors the viewer.
- **Point-of-view** — the camera IS the character's eyes. The viewer discovers what the character discovers. Surprise, first look.
- **Three-quarter view** — character turned three-quarters to the camera. The most common, most readable angle for dialogue and action.
- **Profile view** — full side view. A walk, a journey, a standoff. Two characters in profile facing each other is a confrontation.
- **Front-facing** — character looking straight at the camera. Greeting, lesson, direct address to the child viewer.
- **Rear-facing** — camera behind the character, looking where the character looks. The viewer shares the character's view of what's ahead.
- **Ground-level** — camera at the ground. A small animal's world, a rolling ball, a race. The ground is the stage.
- **Subject-relative angles** — the angle follows what the subject is doing, not the room. A climbing character is shot from below, a falling character from above.

## Shot sizes

- **Extreme wide shot** — the whole world. Where the story happens.
- **Wide shot** — the place and the characters in it. The scene's geography.
- **Full-body shot** — head to feet. A new character, a costume, a walk.
- **Medium-wide shot** — knees up. Action with room to move.
- **Medium shot** — waist up. Conversation and everyday action.
- **Medium close-up** — chest up. A reaction, a decision.
- **Close-up** — the face. The emotion. The most important shot size in kids animation: a child reads a face before a line of dialogue.
- **Extreme close-up** — one detail. An eye, a hand, a key, a smile. The one thing the viewer must not miss.
- **Insert shot** — an object, a note, a button. The clue the story needs.
- **Reaction shot** — a face responding to what just happened. The joke lands here, not in the action itself.
- **Establishing shot** — the first shot of a scene. Where we are, when it is, what the mood is. Every new location gets one.

## Movement

- **Static** — the camera does not move. Comedy timing; moments the viewer must study.
- **Slow push-in** — toward the subject. Attention tightens, emotion grows. The default movement of this system.
- **Pull-out** — back. The world opens up, the scene ends, a surprise is revealed by the wider view.
- **Dolly-in / dolly-out** — smooth forward or back on a track, motivated by the story.
- **Tracking / side tracking / follow shot** — the camera moves with the character. Walk, run, journey. The character stays in frame while the world passes.
- **Pan** — turn left or right. From one character to another, from a face to what it sees.
- **Tilt** — turn up or down. Feet to face, ground to sky.
- **Crane** — rise or fall. Grand reveal, goodbye, a world seen from above.
- **Orbit / arc** — circle the subject. Wonder, decision, transformation.
- **Pedestal** — rise or fall straight up or down without tilting. A character growing taller, a fall.
- **Handheld-style** — slight, imperfect shake. Excitement, running, chase. Use lightly in kids content; too much shake is uncomfortable.
- **Subject-following** — the camera stays with the moving subject. The viewer travels with the character.
- **Virtual camera** — in AI-generated video, the camera is a choice made at generation: the same movements above, described as instructions, held steady across the shot.

## Lens and perspective

- **Wide-angle perspective** — more of the world in frame, close things look bigger. A small room feels big, a character near the camera feels huge.
- **Normal perspective** — the world as the eye sees it. The default.
- **Telephoto perspective** — distant things look close and compressed. A far character feels near, the background flattens.
- **Depth of field** — shallow (subject sharp, background soft) puts the face first; deep (everything sharp) shows the world.
- **Focus pulls** — the sharp point moves from one subject to another inside a shot. The viewer's attention follows the focus.
- **Lens compression, perspective distortion, subject separation, foreground/background depth, camera-to-subject distance** — the tools that separate the subject from the background and tell the viewer where to look.
- **Estimated visual equivalents only.** Never invent exact focal lengths or camera settings from appearance alone. If the actual lens or camera configuration is unknown, label it as an estimated visual equivalent — say what it LOOKS like, not what it WAS.

## Composition

- **Rule of thirds** — the frame in nine parts; the subject on a line or a crossing, not dead centre — unless the moment wants the centre.
- **Central composition** — subject dead centre. Greeting, hero moment, direct address.
- **Symmetry** — both sides mirror. Order, calm, a grand place.
- **Leading lines** — roads, fences, rivers, arms that point the eye to the subject.
- **Foreground framing** — something near (a branch, a doorway, a shoulder) frames the subject and adds depth.
- **Negative space** — empty space around the subject. Calm, loneliness, or room for a big emotion.
- **Character placement and visual hierarchy** — the most important thing in the frame is the biggest, brightest, or sharpest. Never two things competing.
- **Screen direction** — characters move left-to-right on a journey, right-to-left when returning. Hold screen direction across cuts; a character crossing the wrong way confuses the viewer.
- **Headroom and look room** — space above the head, space in the direction the character looks. A character pressed against the frame edge feels trapped; a character with room to look into feels free.
- **Safe zones for vertical video** — in 9:16, keep faces, text, and the main action inside the central safe area. The top and bottom edges are where platform buttons and captions cover the frame.

## The 8-question decision system

For every scene, answer before choosing anything:

1. What is the main story beat?
2. Which character or object should attract attention?
3. Which camera angle communicates that beat most effectively?
4. What shot size is appropriate?
5. Should the camera move — and if so, how?
6. What lens perspective is suitable?
7. What should remain visible in the background?
8. How should the shot transition into the next scene?
