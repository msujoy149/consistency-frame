# Kids Animation — Reference Analysis Methodology

How to analyse a reference video for the `kids-family-animation` skill. This file holds the method — and the honest status of the research so far.

## Status — records exist in the permanent library (2026-11-13)

Real analysis records now exist in the permanent library (`kids-animation-reference-library.md`, records R-01 to R-17) — from videos actually watched in full on 2026-11-13. Videos that could not be played are not recorded; their technique stays out until the user supplies the video files and they are watched in full (see the library's "Pending research" note). Nothing in this file describes a real channel or video. Never invent a channel, a video, a title, a date, a view count, or an observation.

## Rules for every analysis

- Open and watch the actual video if access allows. Analyse the entire video, not just its thumbnail.
- Record the URL and publication date. Record duration and aspect ratio.
- Do not automatically assume that all videos from one channel use the same production method — classify the production method separately for each video whenever possible.
- Study a channel's Shorts separately from its long-form uploads.
- Separate verified facts from visual inference. Do not claim to have watched a video if only its title, thumbnail, description, or transcript was available.
- When direct access is unavailable, request an uploaded video or another accessible reference.
- Do not fabricate timestamps, camera settings, or observations. An uncertain observation is recorded as uncertain, with what is unknown stated plainly.
- Use reference videos to learn techniques, not to reproduce protected creative expression. Never copy exact characters, logos, scripts, dialogue, music, or unique protected visual assets.

## The 25-field record format

For every analysed video, create a structured record with these 25 fields:

1. Reference URL.
2. Channel name.
3. Video title.
4. Publication date.
5. Duration.
6. Aspect ratio.
7. Animation category.
8. Probable production method.
9. Character design.
10. Environment design.
11. Camera angles.
12. Shot sizes.
13. Camera movements.
14. Lens and perspective observations.
15. Lighting.
16. Animation and motion.
17. Facial performance.
18. Dialogue and voice style.
19. Sound design.
20. Editing rhythm.
21. Story structure.
22. Audience appeal.
23. Distinctive production techniques.
24. Techniques reusable in our system.
25. Technical limitations and uncertain observations.

## Timestamped shot breakdown

Where possible, add a timestamped shot breakdown. Format:

```
00:00–00:02 — Opening close-up; character discovers an unexpected object.
00:02–00:05 — Medium shot; character reacts and turns toward the object.
00:05–00:08 — Low-angle action shot; object enters the scene.
00:08–00:12 — Reaction close-up; emotional payoff.
```

The example above is only the format — it describes no actual video. Every timestamp in a real record must come from a video actually watched.

## Per-channel research

For every channel, inspect its channel identity and content categories; examine its recent uploads; identify recurring visual styles, recurring characters, successful storytelling formats, and recurring animation techniques; identify the typical video duration and the target audience suggested by the content; examine its Shorts separately from its long-form uploads.

## What happens with the analysis

Techniques that survive verification go into the permanent reference library (`kids-animation-reference-library.md`), classified into its ten knowledge areas, with the date and source of each discovery recorded — and into the appropriate skill when they belong there. A technique that is only a trend does not become a permanent rule until it is observed, checked against the existing skills, and confirmed to work.
