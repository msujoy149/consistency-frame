# Cinematic Power

An acceptable video and a powerful cinematic one differ in five places: the style is locked before a single shot is made, animals and creatures perform instead of decorating, the edit breathes with the music, a trailer is built as an arc rather than summarized, and scale and colour are scripted for the whole piece. This skill adds those five systems.

Nothing here replaces the existing systems. The visual style bible (`production-systems` skill, A1), script-driven camera angles (A12), editing rhythm (`humanized-production` skill, 30.10), script-driven colour grading (28.7), the character registry (`character-consistency` skill), and the episode QC checklist all still apply, unchanged. This skill adds only the cinematic-power layer on top of that foundation.

## 1. Stylized Animation Style Lock — Choose the Style First

Choose the visual style FIRST, before any generation, and record it in the project (visual style bible, A1). Hold it in every shot of the piece.

Three styles, one choice per project:

- **2D anime.** Consistent line weight, cel colour blocks, fixed highlight and shadow shapes, limited-animation rules: holds used deliberately, motion smears and cycles reused on purpose, never by accident.
- **3D stylized CG.** Fixed proportions, squash-and-stretch, a consistent material look — skin, hair, and cloth read the same in every shot.
- **Photorealistic live-action.** The existing realism rules apply (humanized-production): natural skin, physically accurate light, believable movement.

Background treatment follows the style. A full environment is one choice; a deliberate graphic backdrop is another — a clean colour with floating motifs, used as a design decision, not as an empty background. Either way, the treatment is chosen with the style and held.

Rules:

- Never drift between styles inside one video. A style change is a deliberate, recorded decision — noted in the project record with what changed and why — never an accident of a new generation.
- Every shot is checked against the style lock before delivery: line weight and colour blocks for 2D anime, proportions and materials for 3D CG, realism for live-action.
- The style lock covers characters, animals, objects, and backgrounds alike. A photorealistic character in a cel-shaded world (or the reverse) is a style break, not a creative choice.

**Check:** the style recorded in the project matches the style visible in every delivered shot; no shot drifts into another style.

## 2. Animal & Creature Performance — Creatures Act, They Do Not Decorate

Animals and creatures are registered like characters. Each one is distinct — species, size, colours, markings — recorded in its own record (`<creature-id>`), and held consistent across every shot.

Rules:

- **Movement follows the creature's real body.** Quadruped gait, flight, swimming, crawling — the movement a real body of that shape can make. Never paste human movement onto an animal: no upright walking for a quadruped, no human hand gestures from a paw, no human facial expressions on a muzzle that cannot make them.
- **Creatures act.** A sidekick animal has comic timing: head tilts, eye pops, delayed reactions — the joke lands because the timing is deliberate. A monster has menace in stillness before it moves: it is frightening because it waits, not because it flails.
- **Elemental forces may act as characters.** An ocean, a fire, a storm can behave as a living presence — rising, reaching, withdrawing — with consistent behaviour across the piece. When an element acts, its continuity is checked like any character's: same behaviour, same scale, same relationship to the characters, shot after shot.
- **Groups of animals are never clones.** Each animal in a group is distinct in look and in timing; a group of identical copies reads as a mistake.
- Animals follow the same continuity rules as characters: identity, clothing or markings, and props never change between shots unless the story requires it.

**Check:** every animal and creature in the delivered video matches its record in species, size, colours, and markings, and moves the way its real body moves.

## 3. Music-Beat Editing & Synchronized Ensemble — The Beat Is the Clock

In music-driven videos, the beat is the clock. Build the edit from the song's structure — verse, chorus, drop — and place cuts, gestures, and movements ON the musical beat. The biggest movement lands on the strongest beat.

Rules:

- **One timing reference for the whole ensemble.** When many characters — and animals — move together, they share one timing reference: a synchronized jump, dance, or reaction happens on the same beat for everyone, with no drift between them. Rehearse ensemble timing in the shot list before generation.
- **A group out of sync reads as a mistake, never as style.** If a character or an animal lands a beat late, that is a defect to correct, not a variation to keep.
- **Introductions follow the music.** Introduce characters one by one in time with the music, then bring them together in a group shot, then close with the synchronized finale on the strongest beat. Each section is timed to the song, not to an arbitrary shot count.
- The edit never fights the music: a cut that lands between beats must have a story reason, and a movement that ignores the beat must be deliberate and visible as a choice.

**Check:** every cut and every synchronized movement in the delivered video lands on the musical beat, and the whole ensemble shares one timing with no drift.

## 4. Trailer & Teaser Structure — Built, Not Summarized

A trailer is built, not summarized. It has six beats, in this order:

1. **Open on wonder.** A striking image, no explanation. The audience feels the world before they understand it.
2. **Escalate the stakes.** A threat, a storm, a monster — something that makes the journey necessary.
3. **Reveal the characters.** Each introduction has a job: one shows the hero's determination, one shows the companion's power, one shows the sidekick's comedy. No introduction is decoration.
4. **One comedy beat after tension.** A sidekick, a small creature, a visual gag — placed right after tension so the audience can breathe. Exactly one beat; comedy is a pause, not the trailer.
5. **Climax montage.** Short, fast, escalating — the biggest images in the shortest time, each one raising the last.
6. **End on the title card, then credits.** The title is the last image the audience keeps. Nothing after it competes with it.

Rules:

- Never reveal the ending. Never explain what the images already show — if a shot says it, no line of dialogue needs to say it again.
- Each beat has a duration budget in the shot list. The trailer must work without the full film: a viewer who never sees the film still feels a complete arc.
- A teaser is the same structure shortened: wonder, one stake, one character, title. The order never changes; only the length does.

**Check:** the delivered trailer contains all six beats in order, reveals nothing of the ending, and works as a complete arc on its own.

## 5. Scale, Spectacle & Colour Scripting — Plan Awe Deliberately

Awe is planned, not improvised. Scale contrast is the primary tool for wonder: a small character against a giant wave, volcano, or island makes the audience feel the size of the world. Use it at the beats that need it — not everywhere, or it stops working.

Rules:

- **Spectacle shots are planned per beat in the shot list, never improvised.** Each spectacle shot has a named beat, a named purpose (wonder, threat, triumph), and a camera angle chosen from that beat (A12). A spectacle shot without a beat is decoration.
- **Colour is scripted for the WHOLE piece.** Assign a palette to each emotional beat — bright wonder, warm sunset, dark storm, monster glow, bright title — and plan the transitions between palettes. Colour follows emotion, not only time of day: a storm is dark because the story is dark, and the title is bright because the story resolves.
- **Never change palette randomly inside a beat.** Within one beat, the palette holds; it changes only when the emotion changes, and the change is planned in the colour script.
- Scale and colour work together: the biggest scale contrast lands on the beat with the strongest palette shift, so wonder and colour arrive at the same moment.

**Check:** the delivered video's spectacle shots each serve a named beat, and its palettes change only at planned emotional transitions, never randomly inside a beat.
