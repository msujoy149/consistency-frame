# Emotional Performance

A technically perfect video can still leave the viewer cold. What makes a scene land is the performance inside it: a singer who wins a crowd, a room that turns when a song is sung, two faces held close for minutes while a feeling changes, an old person who remembers, hands that truly play, a song that heals, and a world where a torn photograph can be the whole story. This skill adds the seven systems that make emotional scenes work.

Nothing here replaces the existing systems. The visual style bible (`production-systems` skill, A1), script-driven camera angles (A12), editing rhythm (`humanized-production` skill, 30.10), script-driven colour grading (28.7), the character registry (`character-consistency` skill), one voice per character fixed across episodes (`voice-consistency` skill), the cinematic-power layer (style lock, music-beat editing, scale and colour scripting), and the episode QC checklist all still apply, unchanged. This skill adds only the emotional-performance layer on top of that foundation.

## 1. Musical Performance & Stagecraft — The Song Has an Arc

A live performance on a stage. The performer stands at the microphone and the song has an arc — nervous start, finding the groove, the crowd won over, triumph — and the staging follows it.

Rules:

- **The lighting follows the music.** The stage is a warm island in a cool night: spotlights and coloured washes against the dark. As the song builds, the lighting brightens with it — the stage warms, the washes deepen, the performer is lit more strongly. Lighting that never changes while the song builds is a staging defect.
- **The performer's body loosens as the song builds.** A nervous start is stiff: tight shoulders, a fixed grip on the microphone, small movements. Finding the groove loosens the body: sway, foot tap, the instrument held right, the performer moving with the music instead of against it. Triumph is open: the body takes the whole stage. A performance that never changes gear is a rehearsal, not a show.
- **The crowd's energy rises with the music.** The audience is quiet at the nervous start, leaning in; it warms as the groove is found; it cheers at the triumph. The crowd's energy is the proof the performance worked — see system 2.
- **The stage is built, not empty.** A backdrop, curtains, lights, the instruments on their stands — the stage is a place, and the performer stands inside it. An empty stage with one performer and nothing else reads as unfinished.

**Check:** every stage performance in the delivered video has a visible arc — nervous start, groove, triumph — with the lighting brightening, the performer's body loosening, and the crowd's energy rising along with the song.

## 2. Crowd as a Living Character — Hundreds of Distinct People

A crowd is hundreds of distinct people, never a copy-paste. Each background character is different — face, hat, clothes, age — and each reacts in waves: a gasp spreads, applause rises, the crowd leans in.

Rules:

- **No two crowd members are the same.** Faces, hats, clothes, ages, postures — every background character is distinct. A crowd of repeated faces is a defect the eye finds in seconds.
- **The crowd reacts in waves, never as one block.** A gasp starts in one place and spreads; applause rises in sections; some faces turn first, others follow. The crowd's mood is the scene's barometer: when it turns, the scene has turned.
- **Cut reaction shots between the performer and individual crowd faces.** The performer's triumph is proved by the faces watching, not by the performer alone. Hold on one face long enough to read it — a smile, tears, a hand over a mouth — before cutting back.
- **The crowd has a mood arc of its own.** Skeptical at the start, curious, won over, cheering — record the crowd's mood per beat in the shot list, the same way the performer's arc is recorded.

**Check:** every crowd in the delivered video is made of distinct individuals, reacts in waves rather than as one block, and its mood arc matches the scene's arc — with individual reaction shots proving the performer's triumph.

## 3. Intimate Drama & Emotional Arc — Hold the Faces

A long scene between two people, held for minutes on their faces. The emotional turn is slow — no response, a flicker, recognition, tears, joy — and it is built from micro-expressions, pauses, and silence, not from words.

Rules:

- **Plan the arc beat by beat in the shot list.** What the character feels at each beat, what the face shows, what the other character sees. The turn is written before generation: no response at beat one, a flicker at beat two, recognition at beat three, tears, joy. An emotional turn that is not planned is an emotional turn that does not happen.
- **The camera stays close and still and lets the faces do the work.** Close-ups and medium close-ups, held long, with minimal camera movement. Cutting away to hurry the scene kills it — the scene's power is in the time spent on the face.
- **Dialogue is often less than the face.** When the face says it, the words stop. Pauses and silence are part of the performance, not gaps to fill: a held look, a breath, a hand reaching out. A scene that talks over its own emotional turn is a scene that does not trust the faces.
- **Touch is the emotional climax.** A hand on a cheek, a hug, a hand held — the contact must be real: hands meet, weight transfers, the body responds. Touch follows the multi-character contact rules (`character-consistency` skill, §8) — bodies stay separate and solid, never overlapping — and it lands at the arc's turning point, never before it.

**Check:** every intimate scene in the delivered video holds its faces close and still through a planned beat-by-beat emotional turn, built from micro-expressions, pauses, and silence, with real touch at the climax.

## 4. Age Performance — Age Changes Everything

Age changes everything. Movement, voice, face, mind — the very old perform differently from the adult, and the adult differently from the child, and the contrast is the scene.

Rules:

- **Movement: slow, trembling, frail.** The very old move less, and each movement costs effort: slow steps, trembling hands, a body that rests between movements. A frail character who moves like a young adult is a defect.
- **Voice: slow, thin, frail — and fixed per character.** The old voice is slow and thin; it must match the age and stay the same for that character across episodes — see the `voice-consistency` skill, and its ageing and state variants (system 8). A character's voice never changes silently between episodes; when the story jumps in time, the voice change is deliberate, consistent, and recorded.
- **Face: deep wrinkles, drooping lids, cloudy eyes.** The old face is read in close-up: deep wrinkles, drooping eyelids, eyes that are cloudy or half-closed. The face is never smoothed into youth.
- **Mind: memory loss, then a flicker of recognition.** The very old may not remember — and then something wakes the memory: the eyes open, a smile forms slowly, the voice finds the song. The flicker is the performance: it is slow, it is small, and it is the whole scene.
- **The very old act mostly with their eyes and hands.** A look, a finger tapping, a hand reaching — small movements carry the scene. Do not ask an old character to carry a scene with big body movement; the eyes and hands do it.
- **Different ages perform differently in the same scene.** The elder is slow and still, the adult is strong and fast, the child is quick and loud — and the contrast is the scene. Each age is performed on its own terms, never averaged.

**Check:** every character in the delivered video moves, speaks, looks, and remembers at their own age — the very old slow, trembling, and frail, acting mostly with eyes and hands — and each character's voice matches their age and stays the same across episodes.

## 5. Instrument Performance Sync — The Hands Must Play the Music

When a character plays an instrument, the hands must play the music you hear. Fingers on the frets match the notes; strumming matches the rhythm; the bow, the drumstick, the keys match the sound.

Rules:

- **Close-ups of the hands are checked against the audio.** A finger on the wrong fret while the right note plays is a visible lie. The hand close-up is a QC shot: the fretting hand, the strumming hand, the bowing arm, the keys — each checked against the sound at that exact moment.
- **The instrument is played, not held.** A held instrument that never plays is a prop; a played one is a performance. If the character is shown with an instrument and music is heard, the hands are playing it — visibly, in time.
- **The instrument itself is a hero prop.** The same instrument, the same look, every time it appears: the same colour, the same shape, the same markings, the same wear. An instrument that changes between shots is a continuity defect — record it in the wardrobe and props registry.
- **The instrument is passed, not teleported.** When the instrument changes hands, the handover follows the physical-interaction rules (`video-regeneration` skill): the receiving hand contacts it, it stays between fingers and palm, it is released only after the other hand has secured it.

**Check:** every instrument played in the delivered video is played in sync — fingers, strumming, bowing, and keys match the audio in every hand close-up — and the instrument looks identical in every shot.

## 6. Music as Story & Memory — The Song Is the Plot

The song IS the plot. It unlocks a memory, changes a mind, heals a family. It starts small — one voice, one instrument — and the room joins it emotionally.

Rules:

- **The music is never background: it is the scene's action.** The scene's turning point is the song's turning point. When the song starts, the story moves; when the song ends, the story has moved. A scene where the music plays and nothing changes is a scene where the music was wasted.
- **The song starts small and the room joins it emotionally.** One voice, one instrument — then the room responds: a face softens, a hand taps, a voice joins in. The joining is emotional first, musical second.
- **The family's reaction shots show the song working, each face at a different moment.** Anger softens into tears into a smile — and each person changes at their own moment, never as one block. Each face is a separate shot, held long enough to read.
- **The song's arc is the scene's arc.** Record it in the shot list: the song's beats and the story's beats side by side. The music-beat editing rules (`cinematic-power` skill, system 3) still apply — cuts and movements land on the beat — and here the beat carries the plot.

**Check:** every song that drives a scene in the delivered video starts small, changes the characters one face at a time, and ends with the story moved — the song's turning point is the scene's turning point.

## 7. Cultural Authenticity & Object Storytelling — One Culture, and Objects That Turn the Story

The world is culturally specific and consistent — the festival, the flowers, the banners, the dress, the family house, the food — one culture, held in every shot, never generic. Props carry history and plot: a torn photograph, a hero instrument, a drawer that hides the truth.

Rules:

- **One culture, held in every shot.** The festival decorations, the flowers, the banners, the dress, the house, the food, the altar — all from one culture, chosen once and recorded in the project, and held in every shot. A generic world with one cultural prop pasted on is a defect; a mixed world with props from two cultures is a worse one. The style bible (`production-systems` skill, A1) records the culture with the style.
- **An object can be the story's turning point.** Its damage and its reveal are planned beats — the tear, the missing piece, the drawer opened, the object made whole. Write the object's beats into the shot list alongside the characters' beats: the object is introduced, damaged, hidden, revealed, made whole — each as a planned shot.
- **The object's state must match the story's state at every cut.** Torn stays torn until the story mends it; hidden stays hidden until the story reveals it; whole stays whole after the reveal. An object that is torn in one shot and whole in the next, with nothing in between, is a continuity defect. Record the object's state per shot in the shot list.
- **The hero prop is fixed.** The hero instrument, the photograph, the drawer — the same object, the same look, every time it appears. A prop that changes between shots breaks the story it carries.

**Check:** every scene in the delivered video holds one consistent culture in every shot, and every story object — its damage, its hiding, its reveal, its repair — is a planned beat whose state matches the story at every cut.
